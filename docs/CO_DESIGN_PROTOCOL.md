# Co-design protocol draft

This is a product-design protocol, not a clinical-research protocol. If results will be published as research or used for health/therapeutic claims, obtain the appropriate ethics review first.

## Principle

Children with disabilities/neurodivergent children are design partners, not merely final-stage testers.

## Before a session

- Ask caregivers/educators how the child communicates preferences and refusal.
- Prepare a visual schedule of the session.
- Explain that stopping is always allowed and will not disappoint the team.
- Offer a quiet setting and allow a familiar support person where appropriate.
- Do not require eye contact, speech, or a particular sitting style.
- Keep diagnosis information separate from product preference notes unless it is genuinely necessary.
- Obtain adult consent and age/development-appropriate child assent.

## During a session

Use multiple ways to respond:

- choose between two mockups;
- point/tap;
- arrange cards;
- draw;
- use AAC;
- speak;
- type;
- demonstrate by playing;
- indicate "stop" or "skip".

Observe without treating every behavior as a symptom. Ask concrete questions such as:

- "Muốn nút này to hơn hay giữ nguyên?"
- "Âm này ổn, nhỏ hơn, hay tắt?"
- "Bạn muốn biết trước còn mấy bước không?"
- "Khi sai, bạn muốn trò chơi làm gì?"
- "Gợi ý này giúp hay làm phiền?"

Avoid abstract questions like "Is this accessible?" for young participants.

## Things to test explicitly

### Predictability
- Can the child tell what happens after pressing a button?
- Can they find pause/exit again without relearning the interface?

### Sensory comfort
- Does any animation/audio/color cause withdrawal or distraction?
- Is "calm" actually calm for this child?

### Motor access
- Are targets easy to hit?
- Can the task be completed without dragging?
- Does keyboard/switch scanning need a dedicated mode?

### Language and symbols
- Are icons understood without guessing?
- Are labels easier than icons for some users?
- Does narration help or distract?

### Error experience
- What happens emotionally/behaviorally after a wrong selection?
- Does preserving progress reduce frustration?
- When is a hint welcome?

### Agency
- Can the child pause, skip, or finish without adult intervention?
- Does the interface pressure continuation?

## After a session

Do not reduce feedback to "liked/didn't like." Capture:

- what the child changed;
- what they avoided;
- what they repeatedly returned to;
- preferred sensory settings;
- preferred support style;
- moments of confusion;
- moments of independent recovery.

Invite the child to make one design decision that will appear in the next prototype. Close the feedback loop in the next session by showing what changed.

## Minimum evidence before broad release

Before describing the product publicly as optimized for children with special needs, test with a heterogeneous sample across:

- communication styles;
- motor access needs;
- sensory preferences;
- reading abilities;
- attention/memory profiles;
- ages/developmental stages.

Do not assume one autism/ADHD/dyslexia profile represents the group.
